50 Signatures, One Match: How the Binance EIP-7702 Hack Actually Worked - and Why the Protocol Didn't Fail

The Binance hot-wallet hack looked like Ethereum's new account-abstraction feature failed. It didn't. Here's the real mechanism - a nonce lottery - and the question that actually matters.

On 29 October 2026, an attacker drained a set of Binance hot wallets in one of the largest crypto thefts on record. The headline that spread fastest was that Ethereum's new EIP-7702 feature had been "exploited." That framing is almost exactly backwards, and the gap between what people think happened and what actually happened is the whole lesson. So let's walk it.

What EIP-7702 actually does. Shipped as part of Ethereum's Pectra upgrade, EIP-7702 lets a regular wallet an externally-owned account, the ordinary kind a person controls with a private key temporarily behave like a smart contract. It does this through a signed authorization: the wallet's key signs a message that says "for now, delegate my account's behaviour to this contract." It's a genuinely useful feature (gas sponsorship, batching, recovery). It is also, by design, an enormous amount of power riding on a single signature.

What the attacker did the nonce lottery. Per Blockaid, the security firm that detected and reconstructed the attack, the sequence was methodical rather than brilliant:

The operator funded a fresh address through Tornado Cash and rehearsed the whole delegate-and-sweep routine on wallets they controlled, spending a few dollars each time. They deployed a private "sweeper" contract - unverified, with themselves as the only authorized caller whose job was simply to move tokens and ETH out to an address they owned. Here's the clever part. To delegate the Binance hot wallet to that sweeper, they needed an authorization signed against the wallet's current nonce - and they didn't know what it would be at execution time. So they pre-signed 50 authorizations at once, one for each nonce in a range (18,914,775 through 18,914,824), and fired all 50 in a single transaction. Forty-nine were invalid. One matched. That was enough. The hot wallet was now delegated to the sweeper. A single call moved roughly 167,000 ETH and 1.6 billion USDT. Within about twelve minutes, ten more Binance hot wallets across Ethereum, Arbitrum, Avalanche, Optimism, Bitcoin and Tron sent funds to the same address. Because the delegation stayed in place, the attacker kept sweeping incoming balances for over an hour. Now the part everyone skips. Notice what is not in that sequence: no Ethereum bug, no flaw in EIP-7702, no cryptographic break. The protocol did precisely what it is designed to do - honour a validly signed delegation. The nonce-spray was a neat trick to deal with uncertainty about timing, but it only works if you already have a valid signature from the wallet's key. And that is the one thing Blockaid says it cannot explain: how the attacker obtained a signature from a Binance hot-wallet key. Blockaid's own CEO has publicly pointed at a flaw in signing infrastructure, which remains unconfirmed.

That's the real story, and it's an uncomfortable one for the whole industry: the exploit wasn't in the code. It was in the signature. EIP-7702 didn't lower the bar for attackers who already control signing. What it did was raise the stakes of every signature - because in the 7702 era, one signed delegation no longer authorises a single transfer, it can hand over the entire account.

Why this matters beyond Binance. Account abstraction is turning every ordinary wallet into a programmable one. That's powerful, and it quietly changes the threat model for everyone:

A single signature can now mean "take everything." The classic "just sign this to connect" phishing prompt becomes catastrophic if what you're actually signing is a 7702 delegation. Users and wallets need to show what a delegation really grants before it's signed. Signing infrastructure is now the crown jewels - more than ever. For an exchange, the perimeter isn't just the hot wallet balance; it's every system and process that can produce a signature. HSMs, co-signers, approval workflows and the humans around them are the actual attack surface. Delegations deserve their own monitoring and controls. Type-4 (SetCode) transactions and 7702 authorizations should be treated as high-risk events: require co-signer approval before a delegation is ever signed, allowlist what an account may delegate to, monitor for unexpected delegations, and be able to revoke one fast. The official response - and a caveat. Binance publicly stated that it was investigating the incident. Its subsequent messaging indicated it would make affected users whole and had temporarily paused deposits and withdrawals while it secured systems and worked with law enforcement, pending a full post-mortem - a response that echoes its 2019 "SAFU fund" playbook. We'd stress one thing a security firm always stresses: confirm this on Binance's official channels. In a fast-moving incident, fake "official statements" and impostor support accounts spread faster than the real ones, and at the time of the leading forensic analysis, Binance had publicly confirmed only that it was investigating with no confirmed impact to user funds. Rely on the source, not the screenshot.

A final note on the figures: estimates have ranged from about $2.5 billion for the first sweep to $5.5–6.3 billion across all chains, with large sums reportedly frozen by stablecoin issuers. All of it is preliminary. The number will settle; the lesson won't. In the account-abstraction era, protect the signature like it's the vault - because now it is.
